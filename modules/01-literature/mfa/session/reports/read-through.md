## 1. Lin2022PhishSheep (11b8be0c43685cdd)

**Read:** the whole paper, printed pp. 1651-1668 (PDF 19 pp., about 15 000 words). Sections 1-8 and Appendix A (use cases) read in full. Appendices B-E (lists of fingerprintable properties, canvas fonts, how phishing sites send fingerprints back) only skimmed; nothing in them is used here.

**Method, data, sample.** The authors built two browser extensions, one that extracts a site's own fingerprinting code and one that spoofs a stored fingerprint. They ran differential tests with two devices of their own (a Mac with Chrome as "victim", a Windows laptop with Edge as "attacker") on test accounts or their own accounts, at 16 real services (anonymised by category) chosen from 300 popular sites that run fingerprinting code and support 2FA. They re-tested at least 20 times over six months (04/2021-09/2021). Separately they ran dynamic analysis (VisibleV8) of three phishing-site datasets (71 343, 82 431 and 173 269 sites; JavaScript traces for 39 618, 40 777 and 93 568 of them).
**Grade:** E3 (attack demonstrated, not how often) for the attack part; the phishing-ecosystem part is a measurement of phishing sites, not of accounts, so it is not E2. The paper itself never says how many real victims are affected, and did not collect any real user's fingerprint (p. 1661).

**Verdict on "phishing sites can copy the browser fingerprints it relies on": holds with a narrower wording.** The paper shows that the authors' tool can copy the fingerprints, and that it gets past the second-factor prompt at 9 of 14 real services, under conditions (below). It does not show that phishing sites in the wild generally do this: "phishing sites are not yet widely replicating the fingerprint-generation process of targeted websites" (p. 1661). In the wild, only some sites aimed at a few specific targets collect everything needed. Also, "risk-based authentication" in general is not shown to fall; only the fingerprint-based signal does, and where other signals (IP address, cookies) also count the attack fails.

Conditions and numbers:
- **What the attacker must hold:** the victim's password (the "main threat model assumes that the attacker knows the user's password", p. 1662), obtained by luring the victim to the attacker's page; and the victim must visit that page from the browser/device they normally log in from (p. 1653). The attack is explicitly not for brute-forcing or credential stuffing (p. 1653). So it concerns R3 (phishing, credentials used later), not R1.
- **Services:** Table 2 lists 16 services. For the 14 that trigger 2FA on a new device, the bypass worked at 9 (Bank-A, CreditCard, Trading-A, Trading-B, Tax-B, Tax-C, eCommerce-A, RideSharing, Food&Beverage-A). The five that resisted all require the login IP to match an address used before (p. 1658). Intro says "ten of the 16 (62.5%)" (p. 1652); the tenth is AdBlocking, where 2FA is off by default and the attack only suppresses an e-mail alert (p. 1659). So the 2FA-bypass count is 9 of 14, not 10 of 16. Bank-B and Trading-B use only the User-Agent header, not JavaScript fingerprints (p. 1658).
- **Which second factors:** the attack does not depend on the kind of second factor; it makes the service skip the challenge, including for an authenticator app (eCommerce-A, p. 1661).
- **When it does not work:** where the user has made the service ask for 2FA at every login. RideSharing: "if we explicitly enable 2FA through RideSharing's mobile app, 2FA is triggered for every login attempt" (p. 1667); Trading-A did the same when 2FA was explicitly enabled (p. 1659). Only 8 of the vulnerable services offer such an option, and it is opt-in everywhere (p. 1662). Brave's canvas randomisation made services that use canvas fingerprinting always prompt (p. 1662).
- **Real services?** Yes, real production services, but with the authors' own accounts and no real victims.
- **How common in phishing sites:** of phishing sites that produced a JavaScript trace, 73.98%, 90.08% and 91.36% collect some fingerprint; but this is mostly basic attributes (about 40-49% of all sites in each dataset), and advanced ones (canvas etc.) only 5-7% (p. 1660). Sites with every attribute needed to bypass a given target: Bank-A 89 of 1 098 phishing sites labelled for it over all datasets (8.1%), 74 of 330 (22.42%) in the most recent dataset; Trading-A 6 of 6 in the most recent dataset (Table 5, p. 1660-1661).

**Quotes (all machine-checked):**
1. "we demonstrate how phishing attackers can replicate users' fingerprints on different devices to deceive the risk-based authentication systems of high-value web services (e.g., cryptocurrency trading) to completely bypass two-factor authentication" (Abstract, p. 1651).
2. "our attack can successfully bypass 2FA in 9 out of the 14 websites. The five websites that are not vulnerable to our attack require the device's IP address to match one of the IP addresses previously used to access that account" (Sec. 4.2, p. 1658).
3. "Our main threat model assumes that the attacker knows the user's password." (Sec. 6, Best practices for users, p. 1662).
4. "our findings show that phishing sites are not yet widely replicating the fingerprint-generation process of targeted websites." (Sec. 5, Summary, p. 1661).

**Limits the authors state:** extensions cover only the fingerprinting vectors of popular libraries and sites, custom techniques would need new code (Sec. 6, p. 1661); the phishing analysis covers only sites whose JavaScript they could get ("our analysis is limited to phishing sites for which we were able to obtain their JavaScript code", p. 1661) and client-side cloaking probably hid some; "the attack that we demonstrate may not yet be a widespread threat" (p. 1661); for Bank-B they cannot say how many phishing sites would have bypassed 2FA because they lack the date the IP check was added (p. 1660); the causal claim that phishers adapt to risk-based checks is explicitly not concluded ("we cannot conclusively infer the root cause", p. 1661).
**Limits not stated:** 16 services chosen by hand from a hand-picked list (2fa.directory, personal use), so the 9/14 is not a rate for services in general, and the services are anonymised so nobody can check; one primary and one secondary device only, so cross-OS/browser mismatch was not varied; Table 5 counts for WebInfrastructure (219 of 220 "bypass") are not bypasses of 2FA, since the authors found that service's IP check stops the attack (p. 1659); the vulnerable count is a snapshot, vendors changed behaviour during the study (Trading-A, Tax-B, WebInfrastructure, p. 1659-1660).

**Other points that bear on the four questions, not used in the report:**
- Effect of a second factor (R3, case A1/A2 with risk-based skipping): "Indeed, a study of over 350K real-world hijacking attempts against Google accounts found that device-based 2FA blocks more than 94% of the attempts that originate from phishing attacks [30]." (Sec. 1, p. 1651). Second-hand: it is the authors' citation of another study, so do not credit it to Lin; use only if the original ([30], presumably Thomas et al. 2019, but the paper gives only the number) is added as its own source.
- Sufficiency (R4, case A1): real-time phishing toolkits defeat the code itself: "MITM phishing toolkits like Evilginx [4] allow attackers ... to log into target websites in real time ... and then also trick the victims into divulging a 2FA code, thus allowing the attacker to log into the victim account. However, the major limitation of this attack is that in high-value services that only use short-lived session cookies the attacker can only access the victim's account that one time and would fail in future attempts due to the 2FA challenges." (Sec. 6, "2FA mechanisms", p. 1661; the ellipses join two sentences of one passage). That is the authors' statement without test, so E5-like expert statement at best.
- Necessity of MFA over risk-based authentication (RBA): "fingerprints must be used in conjunction with other signals (e.g., IP address, requiring the presence of specific cookies) to prevent our attack." (Sec. 1, p. 1652): a risk-based scheme with fingerprints alone is not enough; stricter IP checks held.
- Also on RBA as a single-factor alternative: Lin's design is RBA deciding *whether to ask for 2FA*; consistent with the review's point that RBA rations a second factor and does not replace it. The paper adds that 2FA is "typically the last line of defense" (Sec. 1, p. 1652), and the weakness lies in the skip logic, not in the factor.

**Bib check:** authors, title, year, venue (31st USENIX Security Symposium, Aug 10-12 2022, Boston) all match. Missing from the entry: pages (1651-1668), address, and a URL (the paper gives https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/lin-xu). Entry type `@inproceedings` is right. No difference otherwise.

**Proposed replacement sentence in the review:** "A phishing page can copy the browser fingerprints that a risk-based check relies on; in the researchers' tests this made 9 of 14 real services skip the second-factor prompt, given the victim's password, and few phishing sites in the wild yet collect all that is needed \autocite[p.~1658]{Lin2022PhishSheep}."

## 2. Holthouse2025TwentyThreeAndMe (6f67f4463309e31c)

**Read:** the whole paper (arXiv preprint, 8 PDF pages, about 5 500 words, including the reference list; no printed page numbers).

**Method, data, sample.** There is no method section and no data of the authors' own. The paper is a desk analysis of the October 2023 incident from public reports: the company's blog/press statement, trade press (TechCrunch, Bitdefender blog), a vendor blog (Akto) on the missing rate limiting, forum posts, and a lawsuit report, followed by a literature-based discussion of defences. Its own statement of what it does is "This paper provides an in-depth analysis of the 23andMe data breach" (Sec. 1, PDF p. 2).
**Grade:** the review has E3. That is generous: nothing is observed or tested by the authors. The "about 14 000" figure is the provider's own statement relayed (E6 in the review's table: "provider's report without disclosed method"); the paper as a whole is a secondary case description. I suggest "E3 (secondary, E6 for the figure)" and that the sentence names the company as source.

**Where the 14 000 comes from.** From 23andMe: "In a press release from 23andMe, they noted that Golem was able to access less than 0.1%, or roughly 14,000 user accounts, of the existing 14 million 23andMe customers through credential stuffing." (Sec. 1, PDF p. 2). Later: "According to 23andMe's official statements and blog updates, the attackers gained access to approximately 14,000 user accounts, representing less than 0.1% of the company's 14 million customers [16]." (Sec. 3.4, PDF p. 4). Reference [16] is the company's blog post "Addressing data security concerns" (https://blog.23andme.com/articles/addressing-data-security-concerns). The conclusion again says "23andMe's official statements emphasized that only 14,000 accounts were directly compromised" (PDF p. 7). The numbers 5.5 million (DNA Relatives profiles) and 1.4 million (Family Tree profiles) are also company figures, via the same sources.

**Verdict on "A password chosen and reused by the user falls to credential stuffing; one analysed breach began with about 14 000 accounts taken over in that way": holds with a narrower wording.** Points:
- "taken over": the paper says "accessed", "compromised", "directly compromised"; it never says the accounts were taken over (e.g., changed). Use "accessed".
- "chosen and reused by the user": the paper says the attacker used "credentials exposed in other data breaches" (PDF p. 2) and "reused usernames and passwords from previous data leaks" (abstract). It does not say how the 14 000 passwords were chosen. The user's choice of the password is the review's inference from "reused" (fair, but the paper does not ask whether they were also weak).
- The paper is inconsistent on the mechanism: abstract "credential smear attack" (sic), Sec. 3 "brute-force attacks and credential stuffing", conclusion "brute-force techniques and credential stuffing" (PDF p. 7), while the company statement and Sec. 1 say credential stuffing. The review's wording follows the company statement, which is the better-sourced one; say "according to the company".
- "analysed breach": the figure is the company's, not an analysis by the authors.

**What it says on MFA absent or optional.** Less than the bib's CLAIM ("multi-factor authentication was absent").
- Abstract: "the absence of robust security measures such as multi-factor authentication (MFA) and rate limiting". The word "robust" is there; the body never states in plain words that no MFA existed.
- The body says what changed afterwards: "23andMe now requires all new and existing customers to login using two-step verification." (Sec. 1, PDF p. 2); "Following the breach, 23andMe implemented two-step verification as a mandatory security measure [16]." (Sec. 4.4, PDF p. 6; "email-based two-step verification as a long-term security measure", Sec. 4.5). The Fig. 1 timeline gives 6 Nov 2023 for "requires all users to use email two-step verification".
- So the paper supports "was not mandatory before the breach, and was made so after" by implication. It says nothing on whether MFA was offered as an option, nor whether any of the 14 000 had it on. The absence of rate limiting rests on a vendor blog: "According to an analysis by Akto, a company specializing in API security, 23andMe's log-in API did not implement rate limiting ..." (Sec. 3.3, PDF p. 4).

**Quotes (all machine-checked unless marked):**
1. "Initially, approximately 14,000 user accounts were compromised by a credential smear attack, exploiting reused usernames and passwords from previous data leaks." (Abstract, PDF p. 1).
2. "In a press release from 23andMe, they noted that Golem was able to access less than 0.1%, or roughly 14,000 user accounts, of the existing 14 million 23andMe customers through credential stuffing." (Sec. 1, PDF p. 2).
3. "The attack highlights the increasing threat of credential stuffing, exacerbated by poor password hygiene and the absence of robust security measures such as multi-factor authentication (MFA) and rate limiting." (Abstract, PDF p. 1).
4. "Following the breach, 23andMe implemented two-step verification as a mandatory security measure [16]." (Sec. 4.4, PDF p. 6).

**Limits the authors state:** only that the effectiveness of the later measures "ultimately depends on user compliance, particularly regarding password strength and reuse" (Sec. 5, PDF p. 7), and that Golem's claim about wealthy targets "has not yet been confirmed" (PDF p. 2). They do not state any limit of their own method.
**Limits not stated:** no method, no inclusion rule, no verification of the company's figure; reliance on vendor and press blogs; internal inconsistency in the mechanism (above); typos ("credential smear", "credential-surfing") suggest light editing; no comparison with other single-factor accounts, so it cannot show that MFA would have stopped it (the paper's remark "MFA provides a substantial security improvement compared to password-only authentication" PDF p. 6 is an assertion, with a reference to a study of a different kind; the review should not cite it for effect).

**Other points bearing on the four questions:** the paper states "MFA is a critical defense against credential stuffing" (Sec. 4.3, PDF p. 6) and "Nevertheless, MFA provides a substantial security improvement compared to password-only authentication." (Sec. 4.4, PDF p. 6; "password-only" is hyphenated at a line end in the PDF, checked by eye), but gives no evidence of its own; these are assertions, E5-like at best, not usable for RQ effect. It also notes that OTPs "may be intercepted by attackers" with a caveat that this "requires multiple layers of malicious activity" (PDF p. 6, citing Dmitrienko et al.): concerns A1 under R5/R3, second-hand. Rate limiting is named as a separate defence against online guessing (Sec. 4.1): relevant to R1 and to the point that a single-factor case P could be hardened without a second factor. Nothing on necessity or on what each factor contributes.

**Bib check:** authors, title and year match the first page; no venue, date or arXiv stamp is printed in this PDF (it is a Preview export dated 6 Feb 2025), so the eprint number 2502.04303 and the year cannot be confirmed from the PDF itself. `@misc` with `howpublished = {arXiv preprint}` is fitting. Affiliation (Miami University, Oxford, Ohio) is not in the entry; not needed.

**Proposed replacement sentence:** "One analysed breach began, by the company's own count, with about 14 000 accounts that an attacker reached with passwords reused from other services \autocite[Sec.~1]{Holthouse2025TwentyThreeAndMe}; the company made two-step verification mandatory only afterwards." The second clause is optional but is what the paper supports for MFA.
Also fix the CLAIM line: "multi-factor authentication was absent" -> "the paper names the absence of MFA and rate limiting as aggravating and says the company made two-step verification mandatory afterwards; it does not say whether MFA was offered".

## 3. Spooren2015Fingerprinting (ea9ac25b8ead8b73)

**Read:** the whole paper (6 PDF pages, about 5 000 words including references). No printed page numbers: locators are "PDF p. N" and section.

**Method, data, sample.** A field study: test users were invited to open a page (mFinger) on their phones and tablets for one week; the page collected the fingerprint that ForgeRock OpenAM 12's DeviceIdMatch module uses. 69 submissions; one duplicate and 9 desktop/laptop records removed, leaving **59 devices** (Sec. 4.1, PDF p. 3). Measures: number of distinct values and maximum number of identical values per fingerprint component (Table 1); a list of identical fingerprints for named models (Sec. 4.2); and predictability, i.e., how well an attacker who knows brand and model could guess each component, using the 59 records plus about 6 000 user-agent strings from log files (Sec. 4.3). Attributes: screen size and colour depth, time zone, plugins, fonts, user agent, languages and the rest of the navigator object (20 components; Table 1), of which OpenAM matches six (screen, plugins, fonts, time zone, user agent, geolocation; Sec. 3.3).
**Grade:** E4 fits best: the central claim that fingerprints are easily obtained and reproduced is an argument from a stated model (Sec. 5.1), with no attack run; the measurement part is a small convenience sample of devices, not of accounts or incidents, so not E2. The review's "E3/E4 ... Evidence grade: E4" is fine; I would drop "E3".

**Verdict on "on mobile devices those fingerprints are too alike to tell users apart": holds with a narrower wording.** The paper does say something close: "Clearly, for mobile devices, device fingerprinting does not provide enough entropy to uniquely identify the device." (Sec. 5.1) and "the entropy of typical mobile device fingerprints is too small to be used for authentication purposes" (Sec. 6). But:
- The data show duplicates among *devices* of the same or neighbouring models, not that users cannot be told apart: iPhone 4 and 4s identical, iPhone 5 and 5s identical; 2 of 5 Moto G fully identical; 2 of 4 HTC One S identical, 4 of 5 Nexus 5 similar. Everything else in the sample was not shown to be duplicated. The authors' own conclusion from this is hedged: "Although the data collected was very limited, our hypothesis seems to be confirmed: For certain brands and models of mobile devices, the device fingerprint has so little entropy that the added value for its use in risk assessment is questionable." (Sec. 4.2).
- The abstract's own wording is "carry a lot of similarity ... making them less reliable for risk assessment and step-up authentication" (what the bib QUOTE says). That is weaker than "too alike to tell users apart".
- The paper's stronger point is a different one: the components are predictable from the device model (screen size 100%, time zone 100%, plugins 88%, fonts about 91% if the OS is known, user agent about 41%, about 65% with browser known) and the whole fingerprint can be harvested by luring the user to a page and then replayed (Sec. 5.1). That is what "unsuitable for authentication" rests on, and it is the same attack as in Lin et al.
- It is one algorithm (OpenAM 12), one week, 59 devices, a local participant group (the authors say the audience was "localized"); the authors say so (below).

**Quotes (all machine-checked):**
1. "Our research shows that particularly for mobile devices the fingerprints carry a lot of similarity, even across models and brands, making them less reliable for risk assessment and step-up authentication." (Abstract, PDF p. 1).
2. "Out of the 69 respondents whose device fingerprints we collected during a period of one week, 1 duplicate fingerprint was removed, which had an identical cookie and was therefore a resubmitted fingerprint. Additionally, 9 records were removed, which appeared to be desktop or laptop computers, on which our study did not focus – leaving 59 records." (Sec. 4.1, PDF p. 3).
3. "Although the data collected was very limited, our hypothesis seems to be confirmed: For certain brands and models of mobile devices, the device fingerprint has so little entropy that the added value for its use in risk assessment is questionable." (Sec. 4.2, PDF p. 4).
4. "it is built from information which can easily be obtained and then reproduced. It suffices for an adversary to lure a user into visiting a web page controlled by the adversary to obtain the browser's fingerprint. After this, impersonating a user by simulating the user's browser becomes trivial." (Sec. 5.1, PDF p. 5).

**Limits the authors state (Sec. 4.4, PDF p. 5):** "In our field study, we only collected 59 mobile device fingerprints. Although this set contained many of the most common and popular devices, a larger sample set would have given us statistically more significant results."; internal validity: "Some of these had a high predictability due to the localized target audience that participated in our study (e.g. the timezone attribute)."; external validity: "We must be careful with generalizing our results and claiming the validity in other situations, as we chose a particular fingerprinting algorithm as provided by a contemporary identity and access management system, i.e. ForgeRock's most recent OpenAM 12 platform. Other fingerprint algorithms leverage more advanced components, such as HTML5 canvas fingerprints." Also: "In our assessment we did not take into consideration the popularity of mobile devices", and the participants' devices include "more uncommon devices such as smartwatches and Maemo/MeeGo mobile phone variants" (Sec. 4.3, PDF p. 5: "the devices used by our participants is somewhat unbalanced (i.e. more uncommon devices such as smartwatches and Maemo/MeeGo mobile phone variants)").
**Limits not stated:** no statistical measure of uniqueness (no entropy figure, although "entropy" is used in the text); a 2015 browser and a 2015 matching algorithm, so it predates canvas, WebGL and audio fingerprinting that Lin et al. show services now use; the prediction uses the participants' own devices only; the "predictability" percentages are for guessing a component given brand and model, not for matching one user's device against others; no attack was run; no test of how the fingerprint actually affects step-up (no real risk engine measured with real logins).

**Other points bearing on the four questions:**
- RBA as an alternative to MFA (necessity): "At best, it can be used for 'negative authentication', i.e., for determining that a session might be compromised." (Sec. 5.1, PDF p. 5): device context cannot authenticate, only flag. Same conclusion as the guideline quoted in the review ("do not substitute for an authentication factor"), from the 2015 paper's side. It also says "It is therefore our opinion that using device context is not a good authenticator" (an opinion, flagged as such by the authors).
- Privacy tools undermine it: users who deliberately randomise fingerprints "would trigger web applications ... to always boost-up their required authentication mechanism (e.g., to text message based one-time passwords)" (PDF p. 5, in the text just before Sec. 5 or in Sec. 5.1; the two-column layout makes the section uncertain; machine-matched on the page). Bears on RBA's usability, not on security.
- The paper's step-up factors in Fig. 1 are SMS, e-mail and eID: so the step-up factor is a code or an eID (relevant to case A1).
- Nothing on passwords, keys or on sufficiency of a second factor.

**Bib check:** authors and title match (title capitalisation differs: the paper has "Mobile Device Fingerprinting Considered Harmful for Risk-based Authentication"; fine for BibTeX with protected case, or leave). Year 2015 matches (copyright 2015). DOI matches the one printed in the paper (10.1145/2751323.2751329). Venue: the paper's page says "2015 European Workshop on System Security (EuroSec 2015)"; the entry's `series = {EuroSys '15}` and `booktitle = {Proceedings of the Eighth European Workshop on System Security}` are not stated in the PDF, so "Eighth" and "EuroSys '15" cannot be confirmed from it (they presumably come from the ACM record; check there). Pages 1-6: the paper has 6 pages and prints no page numbers, so the range is the PDF page count only.

**Proposed replacement sentence:** "on mobile devices the fingerprints of some models were identical in a small test, and a fingerprint can be harvested and replayed, so they authenticate poorly \autocite[Sec.~4.2, 5.1]{Spooren2015Fingerprinting}." Shorter alternative: "on mobile devices those fingerprints carry little information and are easy to predict".

## 4. NIST2016PIVPrivileged (9bc27c3a2430ba53)

**Read:** the whole text of the body (printed pp. 1-16, PDF pp. 4-19, about 8 000 words in all). Table 1 (control mapping) and Table 2, the acronym list and the references (PDF pp. 20-23) were only skimmed. The PDF is the full 23-page white paper dated April 21, 2016; the correct document.

**Method, data, sample.** No study: an expert guidance document explaining a requirement set by OMB and giving implementation practices. No data of its own. **Grade: E5** (standard or expert consensus: what is required or recommended and the reasons given). Fits.

**Verdict on "An earlier white paper likewise argues for multi-factor authentication of privileged users in place of passwords": holds with a narrower wording.** The paper does set out reasons and does speak of replacing passwords, but:
- **Whose requirement.** The requirement is OMB's, not NIST's: the abstract says the OMB's Cybersecurity Strategy and Implementation Plan (CSIP, 30 Oct 2015) "requires that federal agencies use Personal Identity Verification (PIV) credentials for authenticating privileged users"; Sec. 1 says "the CSIP ... directs agencies to transition to multi-factor ... PIV-based authentication for all privileged users", and "This white paper provides additional information regarding this requirement from the CSIP. The purpose of the white paper is to explain the requirement's importance from a security standpoint and to provide best practices". NIST's own words are recommendations ("This document recommends LOA-4 or LOA-3 PIV authentication for privileged authentication", Sec. 2.3, p. 9). Its general-authority claim ("NIST SP 800-53 requires multi-factor authentication for all systems categorized as MODERATE or HIGH", p. 2) is a statement about another NIST document.
- **Which authenticator.** Not multi-factor authentication in general but PIV: a smart card (or a Derived PIV Credential on a mobile device) holding a cryptographic key, unlocked by a PIN or biometric. "PIV Cards can provide multi-factor authentication by requiring each user to possess a valid card and enter the correct PIN or biometrics for that card." (Sec. 1.2, p. 3). That is the review's case B (key with user verification), and it matches the review's definition of user verification (a local check by PIN or biometric that unlocks a key). Passwords and codes (A1, A2) are not what it recommends. Sec. 1.2 also says that in the Derived PIV Credential the key may be in a software module, giving LOA-3 instead of LOA-4 (Sec. 1.2, 2.3).
- **Reasons (the attacks on passwords).** Sec. 1.1 lists, for password-based single-factor authentication: capturing passwords (keylogger malware, phishing emails and fraudulent websites, recovering stored passwords, monitoring network traffic, shoulder surfing, passwords written down), guessing passwords (default, dictionary), cracking hashes offline, and resetting passwords (intercepting a reset), plus reuse across systems and privilege escalation to administrator level. That matches the review's R1, R2, R3, R5/R8 and L2/L3 and R3 (phishing).
- **On why two factors.** The reason given is that the attacker must get two things: "An attacker would have to compromise two factors—not just one—to gain access, such as something the user has (a smart card) and either something the user knows (a password or PIN to unlock the smart card) or something the user is (a biometric characteristic to unlock the smart card)." (Sec. 1.1, p. 2) and "an imposter must compromise two factors by gaining access to the PIV Card and obtaining the corresponding PIN or biometric to unlock the card" (Sec. 1.2, p. 3). It also says that PIN guessing is "seldom successful against PIV Cards because the card will lock after a small number of failed PIN entry attempts" (footnote 5, p. 3).
- **On the card's key alone, without PIN:** the paper does not compare. It says the key "is stored on the card and protected by active internal security mechanisms. As such, PIV Cards are difficult to compromise." (p. 3), which is about the key's protection, not about adding a PIN. It never asks whether a card without PIN would suffice, and gives no reason why two factors are needed rather than the key alone beyond "two things to compromise". So it gives no evidence for or against case C.
- **Strength of the claim.** "Most instances of user impersonation from password-based single-factor authentication can be prevented by multi-factor authentication." (Sec. 1.1, p. 2) and "so the threats as a whole against password-based single-factor user authentication can only be slightly mitigated" (p. 2). No data, no source for either.

**Limits the authors state:** the practices "assume that other security controls related to privileged user authentication and access are already in place" (Sec. 2.5, p. 13); "it is outside the scope of this white paper to identify which security controls are applicable" (p. 14); the scope is "non-national security federal systems" (Sec. 1, p. 1).
**Limits not stated:** no evidence for any effect; the guidance is for US federal privileged users, with a provisioned PKI and smart cards, not for the public; it predates the phishing-resistant authentication language of OMB M-22-09 and the 2025 revision of SP 800-63B used elsewhere in the review; "single-factor passwords (PINs)" in Sec. 1.2 blurs the PIN as a factor with the PIN as a local check (the card's key is the thing authenticated).

**Other points that bear on the four questions, not used in the report:**
- **Sufficiency (case B, and L/R threats):** the paper itself says PIV does not suffice alone. "An attacker able to gain control of a privileged user's device may be able to hijack privileged access sessions and impersonate that user on critical systems." (Sec. 2.2, p. 8): R7/L3 after authentication. And on the verification architecture: "unlike Kerberos, an attacker could defeat the authentication mechanism by obtaining a copy of the assertion" for SAML bearer assertions (Sec. 2.4.1, pp. 11-12; the sentence runs across the page break), and "The proxy architecture is a less secure approach than the direct or indirect architectures" (Sec. 2.4.2, p. 12): the level of assurance depends on the protocol around the key (R7). This is the review's point that a second factor does not remove session theft; here for case B.
- **Sufficiency and necessity:** the strength of the factor depends on how the key is held: "Derived PIV Authentication certificate ... depending on whether the private key corresponding to the credential is protected and used in a hardware or software cryptographic module" (Sec. 1.2, p. 3; machine-checked fragment of a longer sentence). Hardware vs software key, case B.
- **R1 and R3 for case B:** "The deployment of PIV Cards is an important part of the Federal Government's effort to mitigate theft and subsequent reuse/replay of users' credentials." (Sec. 1.2, p. 3; machine-checked). The card's key does not leave the card, so there is nothing to phish and replay; the paper says this about capture and replay but does not discuss real-time relay (R4).
- **Attribution (what each factor contributes):** a general statement only: "an imposter must compromise two factors". No separate contribution of the PIN beyond locking after few attempts (fn. 5).
