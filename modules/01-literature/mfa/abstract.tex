Multi-factor authentication is widely recommended, and often required,
for the authentication of users.
We ask whether the literature shows it to be sufficient or necessary
for security, and whether its benefit comes from combining factors or
from the strength of one of them.
The review is a rapid review: questions, predicted answers and method
were fixed before a documented search of five databases, in both
directions for each question; the search was then repeated without a
limit on the number of records and extended by three queries.
In all, 75 sources are cited, a quarter of them read only as
abstracts.
Measurements of real accounts consistently show less compromise with a
second factor, but the size of the effect is not well established.
Multi-factor authentication is not sufficient: codes and push
approvals are defeated in use, and accounts protected by cryptographic
keys can be entered around the key.
It is not shown to be necessary against remote attackers: what protects
there is a key bound to the service, with or without a password beside
it, although no study tests such a key alone.
A second factor does work of its own only against loss of the device,
and then only if the device does not hold it.
The conclusions on attribution and necessity rest on analysis and
assessment, not on measurement.
